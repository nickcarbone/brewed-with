Cooking with beer

Beer turned out to have a smaller place in the world's cooking than this book first assumed, and finding twelve honest dishes took some looking. Where beer does go into the pot, it tends to arrive by one of two routes, and the dishes here sort neatly between them.

The first route is the old brewing belt of northern Europe, where beer was the everyday drink and simply took the place wine held further south. The Routard guide puts the divide in northern France plainly: beer in the north, and cream and butter further south.31 In Flanders, Bohemia, Bavaria, England and Ireland, the dark, malty beers people drank became the liquid for long braises and roasts, which is why so much of this book is beef cooked slowly in brown beer. Belgium's beer culture, now on UNESCO's heritage list, explicitly includes cooking with beer.17

The second route follows lager as it spread in the 19th and 20th centuries. German brewers who left after 1848 built Milwaukee's industry.36 Mexico's first lager was brewed in 1869 at a brewery founded by Emil Dercher, and Monterrey's Cervecería Cuauhtémoc followed in 1890.43 Cusqueña was founded in Cusco in 1908 by Ernesto Günther.48 Under French rule, Vietnam's first breweries opened in Saigon in 1875 and Hanoi in 1890.52 Korea's first brewery opened in Seoul in 1908.56 Where lager arrived late, it entered the kitchen as a light, cheap liquid: for poaching sausages, steaming shrimp, boiling pork or loosening a pan. The beer in those dishes is often recent and sometimes optional, and the recipes say so.

The gaps matter too. Africa has some of the oldest beer traditions, the sorghum and millet beers brewed across the continent for longer than written records go back.58 They are drunk while still fermenting,57 and are hard to find outside the places that make them. I found no documented savoury dish cooked with them, and lager-era cooking in southern Africa survives mostly as beer bread. Oceania has the same story with damper. Beyond China, Vietnam and Korea, Asian cooking makes little use of beer, and the Chinese beer duck was dropped because duck is hard to buy in a US supermarket. The result leans on Europe, which is an honest picture of where beer cooking lives.

What the beer does in the pot depends on its style more than its colour. Malt gives sweetness and body, and darker, roasted malts add depth. Hop bitterness is the one thing to watch, because it does not cook away. The style guide below explains which beers to use and the one to keep out.

Alcohol and children

Cooking does not remove all the alcohol from a dish. The USDA measured how much stays behind after common methods, and the figures below are its retention factors, the share of the original alcohol left in the food.12 Long, stirred simmering removes the most. Brief cooking, liquid kept hot and served, and anything baked without stirring keep much more.

Beer is weak to start with, so the absolute amounts here are small. The last column estimates the grams of alcohol in one serving at each recipe's retention band, assuming the ABV listed and that the serving carries its share of the pot. One US standard drink contains about 14 g.3 How much a child should have, if any, is a call for parents, and every recipe gives a non-alcoholic option.

Beer sold as non-alcoholic in the US may legally contain up to 0.5% alcohol by volume; only beer labelled alcohol-free has none.4

Beer styles for cooks

Colour is given in SRM, the brewers' scale, from about 2 for straw-pale lager to 40 for black stout. Bitterness is given in IBU. For cooking, bitterness matters more than colour. Ranges are from the BJCP 2021 style guidelines.

American lager

SRM 2–3.5 (straw) · IBU 8–187

Light and barely bitter. The safe choice for poaching, steaming and boiling, where the beer flavours food without reducing.

In the shop: Miller High Life, Pabst Blue Ribbon

Used in: Beer brats, Tôm hấp bia sả, Maekju suyuk

International pale lager

SRM 2–6 (straw to gold) · IBU 18–258

Crisp and bready. Good for quick sauces and deglazing, and reduces cleanly for a short time.

In the shop: Heineken, Tecate, Super Bock

Used in: Francesinha, Discada norteña

Dark lager (Czech dark, Munich dunkel)

SRM 14–35 (brown to black) · IBU 8–3410

Caramel and toast with little bitterness. Makes a sweet, bready gravy and holds up to long cooking.

In the shop: Shiner Bock, a Bavarian dunkel, Kozel Dark

Used in: Pivní guláš, Schweinsbraten, Arroz con pollo

Belgian dubbel

SRM 10–17 (amber to brown) · IBU 15–2516

Dark fruit and soft malt. The best all-round braising beer, because its low bitterness survives hours in the pot.

In the shop: Chimay Première, Leffe Brune

Used in: Stoofvlees

Oud bruin (Flemish brown)

SRM 17–22 (brown to dark brown) · IBU 20–2514

Malty with a gentle sourness, which gives stews their sweet-sharp finish. Rare in US shops; a dubbel with a spoon of vinegar comes close.

In the shop: Uncommon in US supermarkets

Used in: Stoofvlees

English brown ale

SRM 12–22 (amber to dark brown) · IBU 20–3011

Nutty and dry. Makes a classic gravy with enough bitterness to cut rich meat.

In the shop: Samuel Smith's Nut Brown Ale

Used in: Steak and ale pie

Irish dry stout

SRM 25–40 (dark brown to black) · IBU 25–4512

Roasted and bitter. Use one can at most and balance it with tomato or a little sugar.

In the shop: Guinness Draught

Used in: Beef and stout stew

Bière de garde

SRM 6–19 (gold to brown) · IBU 18–2815

Malt-forward farmhouse beer with modest bitterness. Rounded and good with chicken and cream.

In the shop: Rare; Leffe Blonde or Samuel Adams Boston Lager as stand-ins

Used in: Coq à la bière

American IPA: keep it out of the pot

SRM 6–14 (gold to brown) · IBU 40–7013

Hop bitterness does not boil away. As water and alcohol leave the pot, the bitter compounds stay behind, so every spoonful of a reduced sauce is more bitter than the beer was. A 40–70 IBU beer cooked down by half tastes harsh and medicinal. Every recipe in this book uses a low-bitterness style for that reason.

Beef and a great deal of onion, cooked slowly in brown beer until the sauce turns glossy and faintly sour-sweet.

Stoofvlees, also called stoverij in Dutch and carbonade flamande in French, is the Flemish beef-and-onion stew made in beer. It belongs to Belgium, the Netherlands and French Flanders alike, and it is a fixture of Belgian friteries, where it comes with a cone of fries. Its defining ingredients are beef, beer and mustard, with the sauce often thickened by a slice of bread spread with mustard and left to dissolve on top of the pot. Cooks in East and West Flanders add prunes, and some swap the bread for gingerbread or speculaas biscuits.18

The beers named for it are oud bruin (old brown), brown abbey beer and Flanders red.18 That choice tells you most of what the beer does here. These are malty, low-bitterness beers, and the old browns carry a gentle sourness, so the pot reduces to something sweet, dark and sharp at once, which is why recipes finish with a spoon of vinegar and one of sugar. The beer is the whole cooking liquid, and nothing about that role is new.

Belgium's beer culture was added to UNESCO's list of intangible heritage in 2016. UNESCO's own citation counts almost 1,500 Belgian beers and names cooking with beer as part of the tradition.17 You will often read that stoofvlees is medieval, or that it dates from the 18th century. I found no documentation for either claim, so treat them as folklore.

Checked. Beer, mustard and bread are confirmed as the core of the dish. Medieval and 18th-century origin dates are undocumented and left out.

The beer

A Belgian dubbel (SRM 10–17, IBU 15–25) gives dark-fruit malt and very little bitterness, so it survives three hours of reduction.16 An oud bruin (SRM 17–22, IBU 20–25) is the more traditional pick and adds a mild sourness.14

On the shelf. Chimay Première (the red label) and Leffe Brune are the dubbels most often on US supermarket shelves. Flemish browns are uncommon; if you find one, use it.

Avoid. Any IPA or hop-forward pale ale. American IPAs run to 40–70 IBU, and that bitterness concentrates as the liquid cooks down.13

Method

  1. Pat the beef dry and season it well with salt and pepper. Heat the oil and 1 tbsp butter in the Dutch oven over medium-high heat.
  2. Brown the beef in three batches, 3–4 minutes a side, until each piece has a dark crust and lifts off the base without sticking. Crowding the pot makes it steam grey, so keep the batches small. Set the meat aside on a plate.
  3. Lower the heat to medium. Add the remaining butter, the onions and a pinch of salt. Cook for about 15 minutes, scraping up the browned bits, until the onions are soft and a deep golden brown.
  4. Stir in the flour and cook for 1 minute. Pour in the beer a little at a time, scraping the base as it foams. Add the sugar, vinegar, bay and thyme, then return the beef and its juices. The liquid should come most of the way up the meat; top up with a splash of water if it doesn't.
  5. Spread the mustard thickly over one side of each bread slice and lay them on the surface, mustard side down. Cover.
  6. Simmer on the lowest heat for about 2½ hours, or move the pot to a 300°F (150°C) oven. Stir every 40 minutes or so. The bread will collapse into the sauce. The beef is ready when a cube breaks apart under the side of a wooden spoon.
  7. Uncover and simmer for 10–15 minutes if the sauce is thin. It should coat the spoon and look glossy and mahogany. Taste and adjust with a little more vinegar or sugar.

Serve. Fries are the Belgian standard, baked on the sheet tray while the stew finishes, with mayonnaise. Boiled potatoes work too.

If you don’t have it

No dubbel
Any Belgian brown or a malty English brown ale works. The brown ale makes a drier, plainer stew and moves it towards an English braise.
No bread
Four speculoos biscuits (Lotus Biscoff in the US) are a recognised Flemish variant. The sauce turns sweeter and gently spiced.
Non-alcoholic
Use a non-alcoholic dark beer such as Guinness 0.0, with an extra teaspoon of vinegar. You keep the colour and some roast, but lose the dubbel's fruit. Stock alone makes a decent onion stew that no longer tastes Flemish.

To drink with it

The same dubbel. Chimay Première or Leffe Brune are the easy US options.

Leftovers

Better the next day. Keeps 4 days in the fridge and freezes well for 3 months.

Alcohol. Retention band 5%. Simmered and stirred for about 2½ hours (USDA code 5009). That is roughly 0.3 g of alcohol per serving, against 14 g in one US standard drink.3

The Czech pub stew of beef, onion and paprika, deepened with a bottle of dark lager.

Czech guláš descends from Hungarian gulyás, the herdsman's dish, but the two have parted ways. The Hungarian version is a soup-stew with potatoes cooked in the pot. The Czech one is a thick beef stew served alongside bread dumplings (knedlíky). In pubs it comes topped with slices of raw onion, with a beer next to it, and beer can also go into the pot.19 Czech newspaper food pages treat dark-beer goulash as a named variant with its own recipes.20 That makes the beer common but optional here, and the book marks it as contested.

The country gave the world its first pale lager. Josef Groll, a Bavarian brewer hired by the burghers of Plzeň, served the first batch of what became Pilsner Urquell on 11 November 1842.21 Czechs still drink more beer per head than anyone else: 148.8 litres in 2024, the 32nd year in a row at the top of Kirin's world ranking.22 The beer in this pot is the other Czech tradition, the dark lager (tmavé pivo), which brings roast and caramel without much bitterness.

Onion does most of the work. Czech cooks use close to the same weight of onion as beef, browned slowly until it melts into the sauce.

Checked. The pub serving with raw onion and beer, and beer as an optional addition, are both confirmed. The Pilsner and consumption figures are sourced. Beer in the pot is marked as contested.

The beer

Czech dark lager (SRM 17–35, IBU 18–34) adds colour and caramel malt with moderate bitterness.9 One bottle is enough; the dish does not need more.

On the shelf. Kozel Dark turns up in some US beer aisles. Shiner Bock is the widely stocked fallback, and any Munich dunkel works.

Avoid. Hoppy beers, including a Czech pale lager in quantity. Czech premium pale lagers run to 30–45 IBU and turn harsh over two hours.9

Method

  1. Heat the oil in the Dutch oven over medium heat. Add the onions and a pinch of salt and cook for about 20 minutes, stirring often, until they are dark gold and jammy. This is the step that makes the goulash, so give it the full time.
  2. Turn the heat to medium-high, add the beef and cook for 8–10 minutes, turning, until the outside of the cubes has lost all its pink and is browning in places.
  3. Take the pot off the heat and stir in the paprika. Paprika burns quickly and turns bitter, so do this away from the flame. Add the tomato paste, garlic and caraway, if using, and stir for 30 seconds.
  4. Return to the heat, pour in the beer and let it bubble for 2 minutes, scraping the base. Add stock until the meat is just covered.
  5. Cover and simmer gently for 2 hours, stirring now and then and adding a splash of water if the level drops below the meat. It's ready when the beef is tender enough to cut with a spoon.
  6. Whisk the flour with 3 tbsp of the cooking liquid, stir it back in and simmer uncovered for 10 minutes until the sauce is thick enough to coat a spoon. Season with salt.

Serve. With crusty bread or boiled potatoes, and a few rings of raw onion on top, as in a Czech pub. Shop-bought knedlíky are not sold in US supermarkets, and bread is the honest substitute.

Beef braised in brown ale under a puff-pastry lid, the pub pie of England.

The steak pie is a British standard: stewing steak and gravy in pastry. Steak and ale is the pub version, using one of many ales as the braising liquid; in Ireland the same idea with stout and bacon is called steak and Guinness pie.23 It is pub food in the plainest sense, served with mash or chips and peas.

Ale here is the cooking liquid and becomes the gravy. Claims that the pie descends directly from medieval recipes of beef in ale baked in pastry are common online, but the versions I found paraphrase old sources loosely and I could not verify them. I also found no reliable date for when steak and ale became a named pie. Treat both as unproven.

What is documented is the pub culture around it. The Campaign for Real Ale was founded in 1971 to fight the spread of mass-produced keg beer and the loss of local brewing, and it now lists about 4,500 real-ale outlets in its Good Beer Guide.24

Checked. The dish and its Irish stout cousin are confirmed. Medieval ancestry and any naming date are unverified and left out.

The beer

English brown ale (SRM 12–22, IBU 20–30) is nutty and malty, with enough bitterness to cut the richness but not enough to turn harsh.11

On the shelf. Samuel Smith's Nut Brown Ale is often stocked; Fuller's London Pride, a bitter, also works well.

Avoid. IPAs. Their 40–70 IBU concentrates in a reduced gravy and tastes medicinal.13

Method

  1. Toss the beef with the flour and a good pinch of salt and pepper. Heat the oil in the Dutch oven over medium-high heat and brown the beef in three batches until crusted, 5–6 minutes per batch. Set aside.
  2. Lower the heat to medium, add the butter, onions and mushrooms and cook for 8 minutes, until the mushrooms have given up their liquid and are browning.
  3. Stir in the tomato paste for 1 minute. Pour in the ale, scraping the base, then add the stock, Worcestershire, thyme, bay and the beef with its juices.
  4. Cover and simmer gently for 2 hours, stirring occasionally, until the beef is tender and the gravy is thick enough to coat the spoon. If it is thin, simmer uncovered for 10–15 minutes.
  5. Cool the filling for at least 30 minutes. A hot filling melts the pastry before it can rise. Heat the oven to 400°F (200°C).
  6. Spoon the filling into the baking dish. Unfold the pastry, roll it slightly so it overhangs the rim by about ½ inch (1 cm), lay it over, press the edges onto the rim and cut two slits in the middle for steam. Brush with egg.
  7. Set the dish on the sheet tray to catch drips and bake for 30–35 minutes, until the pastry is puffed and deep golden and the gravy bubbles at the edges. Rest 5 minutes before serving.

Serve. Mashed potatoes and peas.

The Irish pub stew of beef, root vegetables and a can of dry stout.

Irish stew in its traditional form is lamb or mutton with root vegetables, and purists allow only neck chops of mutton, or kid.25 The first published Irish stew recipe, in Cassell's Dictionary of Cookery in 1874, used mutton.26 Beef and stout is a different dish, and its history is contested. Nobody I could find has traced when stout went into the pot, though most Irish pubs now serve both versions.26 It is in this book as the dish most people mean by a Guinness stew, with that caveat attached.

The stout is the reason it exists. Arthur Guinness took the lease on the brewery at St James's Gate in Dublin in 1759. Roasting part of the barley gives the beer its colour and dry, roasted flavour, and since 1959 the draught version has used nitrogen for its creamy texture.27 Ireland ranks fourth in the world for beer drunk per head.22

Roasted grain brings bitterness, so the recipe balances it with tomato paste and a pinch of sugar, and keeps the reduction gentle.

Checked. Lamb or mutton as the traditional meat and the 1874 mutton recipe are confirmed. The origin of beef with stout is unknown and is marked as contested.

The beer

Irish dry stout (SRM 25–40, IBU 25–45) gives colour and a roasted, coffee-like depth.12 It is more bitter than the other braising beers in this book, so use one can and no more.

On the shelf. Guinness Draught in the nitro can is in almost every US beer aisle.

Avoid. Imperial, coffee and dessert stouts, which are strong and sweet and take over the pot. Guinness Foreign Extra is noticeably more bitter than Draught.

Method

  1. Blend the coriander with the half yellow pepper, the jalapeño and ½ cup (120 ml) of the stock until completely smooth.
  2. Season the chicken. Brown it skin side down in the oil in the Dutch oven over medium-high heat, about 6 minutes, then 2 minutes on the other side. Set aside.
  3. Lower the heat to medium. Cook the chopped red onion, garlic and cumin for 5 minutes until soft.
  4. Add the coriander paste and fry it for 3–4 minutes, stirring, until it darkens from bright to deep green and smells cooked.
  5. Pour in the beer and boil for 2 minutes. Add the rest of the stock, return the chicken and simmer, covered, for 15 minutes.
  6. Stir in the rice, carrots, corn and pepper strips, pushing the rice under the liquid. Bring back to a simmer, cover tightly and cook on the lowest heat for 20 minutes, without lifting the lid.
  7. Scatter the peas over, cover again, take the pot off the heat and rest for 5 minutes. While it rests, toss the sliced red onion with the juice of 2 limes and a good pinch of salt. That is the salsa criolla. Fluff the rice with a fork and serve with the salsa and lime wedges.

Serve. Salsa criolla is part of the dish. Peruvian cooks often add papa a la huancaína on the side.

Shell-on shrimp steamed over beer and bruised lemongrass, with salt, pepper and lime to dip.

Hấp bia, steaming with beer, is a whole family of Vietnamese dishes. Vietnamese cooking sites list sixteen or more, among them shrimp, fish, crab, chicken and pork knuckle.50 They are nhậu food, eaten with friends over drinks, and shrimp with lemongrass is the simplest of them. Beer is poured over the shrimp in the steamer and perfumes them as they turn pink.51 No origin is documented, and the method is plainly modern.

Beer itself arrived with the French. The Vietnamese word bia comes from bière.53 Victor Larue opened a brewery in Saigon in 1875, the start of what became Sabeco and Bia Saigon. Alfred Hommel opened Hanoi's first brewery in 1890, which became Habeco, the maker of Bia Hà Nội.52 By 2019 the country was producing 4.4 billion litres a year.53

You don't need a steamer for this. The Dutch oven, a bed of lemongrass and a tight lid do the job.

Checked. The dish and method are confirmed in Vietnamese recipe sources. The brewery dates are sourced. No origin claim is made.

Method

  1. Trim the lemongrass, cut it into 3-inch (8 cm) lengths and bruise them with the back of a knife. Lay them over the base of the Dutch oven with the garlic and half the chilli.
  2. Rinse and pat dry the shrimp, and spread them on the lemongrass. Season lightly with salt.
  3. Pour the beer over the shrimp, cover tightly and set over high heat.
  4. Steam for 6–8 minutes, shaking the pot once, until every shrimp has turned pink and curled into a loose C. A tight O means it is overcooked.
  5. While it cooks, make the dip (muối tiêu chanh): a spoonful of salt and plenty of black pepper in a saucer, with the juice of a lime squeezed in at the table and the rest of the chilli.
  6. Tip the shrimp onto a platter with the lemongrass and a spoon of the pan juices. Peel at the table and dip.

Serve. Steamed jasmine rice and a plate of lettuce, cucumber and herbs.

Pork belly boiled in beer and aromatics, sliced and wrapped in leaves with kimchi.

Suyuk is boiled meat, and bossam is pork belly boiled, sliced thin and wrapped in leaves with kimchi and garlic. It is often eaten as anju, food that goes with drink, and it is traditionally tied to gimjang, the autumn kimchi-making, when the household ate boiled pork with the fresh new kimchi.54 The boiling liquid is seasoned to cut the porkiness, with doenjang, coffee, tea or ginger.54

Beer is one of those options. Korea's biggest recipe site carries maekju suyuk as a home dish: the pork is seared, then boiled in a can of beer with onion, garlic and bay, uncovered at first so the alcohol and the porky smell go off together.55 It is modern and optional, and this is the book's least traditional entry, included because it is how many Korean home cooks make the dish now.

Beer came to Korea in the early 20th century, and Seoul's first brewery opened in 1908. The two companies that became Hite and OB were both founded in 1933.56

Checked. The dish, its serving and the gimjang link are confirmed. Beer as the boiling liquid is a documented modern home method and is marked as recent.

Method

  1. Cut the pork into two or three pieces that fit the pot lying flat, each about 2 inches (5 cm) thick.
  2. Sear the pork in the dry Dutch oven over medium-high heat, about 3 minutes a side, until golden on all faces.
  3. Add the onion, 6 garlic cloves, ginger, spring onions, peppercorns and bay. Pour in the beer and enough water to come halfway up the pork.
  4. Boil uncovered on high for 10 minutes. Then cover, turn to medium-low and simmer for 25–30 minutes, until a chopstick slides into the thickest piece with little resistance.
  5. Turn off the heat and leave the pork in the covered pot for 10 minutes to settle.
  6. Mix the sesame oil with a good pinch of salt and black pepper for the dip. Slice the pork across the grain about ¼ inch (5 mm) thick.
  7. To eat, lay a slice in a leaf with a little kimchi and a slice of raw garlic, dip, wrap and eat in one bite.

Serve. Rice and a bowl of soup make it a full meal.
